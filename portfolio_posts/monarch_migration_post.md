## Annual Migration of Monarch Butterflies

<embed type="text/html" src="monarch_migration_map.html" width="800" height="800">

*Interactive map of global monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus) observations over the course of a year, 2023. Data acquiried from the [Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF)](https://www.gbif.org/).*





Every year, monarch butterflies (Danaus plexippus) make their seasonal overwintering migrations from the [Northern United States, as well as some parts of southern Canada, to  Central Mexico and Southern California](https://www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/Monarch_Butterfly/migration/index.shtml). This interactive map displaying the monarchs migration patterns in 2023 was generated using data collected from the [Global Biodiversity Information Facility](https://www.gbif.org/), which relies on both global databases and citizen observations to track species over time. As a result, the map is consistent with the distribution and migration patterns observed in [previous research](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2019.00362/full), with a dominant North American population group migrating south in the winter, and less populous groups in Western Europe, Eastern Australia, and New Zealand primarily remaining within the same region. 

While most North American monarchs migrate to [avoid fatal freezing temperatures and find suitable milkweed plants for their larvae to reproduce](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(18)30253-7), some groups remain consistently in Mexico and Southern California as their constantly warm climates negate the need to migrate. Australian and New Zealand monarchs do not migrate very far for the same reason, though some migrate northwards up the coast and even reach parts of New Guinea. 

Monarchs know when and where to migrate based on a fascinating internal system which detects changes in sunlight, climate, and even Earth's magnetic field. While this may sound like magic, [researchers](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(18)30253-7) have determined what drives this internal GPS. This system relies on the collaboration of what is called a [bidirectional time-compensated sun compass and a light-dependent inclination magnetic compass for orientation, as well as environmental factors and interactions with other monarchs](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(18)30253-7). Together, these internal functions and external interactions guide monarchs through their seasonal migration every year for the sake of breeding, highlighting the power of evolution.
